Two domains, one detector, and an energy balance that closes

Spectra are compressed on the sensor and rebuilt in the cloud. Everything below is measured on real bearing data: the acceleration rail carries harmonic combs, the envelope rail carries modulation, and every bin of energy is assigned to exactly one owner so that loss can be stated as an energy error rather than an uninterpretable distance.

01Why two domains

Modulation shows up in an acceleration spectrum as sidebands: matched pairs straddling a carrier. Finding them there means testing candidate spacings against a grid whose bins, at 0.039 orders, are wider than the separation being tested once the spacing drops below about half an order. Windows for adjacent sideband slots overlap, and a wrong spacing scores well by borrowing the right line.

Demodulation removes the problem instead of fighting it. The envelope of a band-passed signal has the modulation at baseband, where a sideband pair becomes a single isolated line. A comb in the envelope rail is a modulation frequency. So one detector, the comb finder, runs on both rails and there is no separate sideband search at all.

Measured consequence: modulation energy survives the codec at 0.024 dB median error with 0.000% frequency error, against 0.94 dB for acceleration combs. Envelope lines are discrete and sit clear of the comb structure that defeated the sideband search.

03Nothing is dropped on the floor

A comb-only model throws everything it cannot explain into a residual and stops tracking it. That loses exactly the content a reliability engineer cares about most: a lone unbalance line, a vane or blade pass, a broadband hump growing in one region. Four pattern kinds now claim energy, in order of specificity, and each bin has one owner so the parts never exceed the whole.

On controlled tests the residual fell from roughly 30% to 0.7–6.1% once peaks and haystacks were added. A vane pass at 7x, a blade pass at 11x and a broadband hump over 60–95x were all recovered as their own tracked patterns. A lone 1x spike is not reported separately, and should not be: it is the comb's own fundamental, and its growth is visible as the comb's energy rising.

04Pattern energy through the codec

The loss question is not whether bins match. It is whether the pattern layer, run on the rebuilt spectrum, sees the same patterns carrying the same energy. So the identical extraction runs on both sides and the results are compared pattern by pattern.

05Ontology view speed

The kinematic expansion is a view over the asset model: shafts, bearings, gear meshes and passing elements crossed with their harmonics. Convenient, and it means no query anywhere contains a bearing multiplier. But it is a wide cross join, and the cost shows.

Query latency, view against materialised table

The view costs about 83 ms and the materialised table 0.63 ms — roughly 130x. More telling, view latency is flat from 8 to 128 assets while the row count grows sixteen-fold. The cost is the expansion itself, not the rows it returns, so it is paid in full on every call no matter how small the answer. Materialise it and refresh on asset change, which is rare.

06Rising patterns, by family

Because each pattern carries a stable key and an energy, a pattern is a time series. Ranking by rise against a pattern's own early history gives the anomalously growing content directly, with no thresholds on absolute level and no asset model needed. Joining that ranking to the kinematic expansion labels each riser by family.

What the family join buys, and what it does not

Honest limit: pattern observations currently average two per pattern, because only transmitted acquisitions carry spectra and the gate keeps those to about 13% of running acquisitions. Rise statistics over two points are not statistics. The fix is already specified and cheap — send the 26-byte change fingerprint on every running acquisition and leave full spectra gated — but it is not yet built, so treat the rise column as directional.
